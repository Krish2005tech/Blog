For the past few days, I’ve been learning something new **reinforcement learning (RL)** and honestly, it has been one of the most interesting shifts in perspective I’ve had while studying machine learning.

During my **4th semester PRML course**, I was introduced to the foundations of ML: supervised and unsupervised learning. We covered linear and logistic regression, Bayesian decision theory, decision trees, random forests, PCA, LDA, k-means, GMMs, SVMs, and later neural networks like CNNs and RNNs.

All of these approaches, in one way or another, focus on *learning from a fixed dataset*.

As one might guess, the topic that naturally comes next at least according to me is **reinforcement learning**. And it turns out to be fundamentally different from everything that comes before it.

---

## Supervised, Unsupervised, and Reinforcement Learning (in short)

- **Supervised Learning**  
  Learns from labeled data. Given input–output pairs $(x, y)$, the goal is to learn a mapping $f(x) \approx y$.

- **Unsupervised Learning**  
  Works with unlabeled data and aims to discover structure or patterns such as clusters or latent variables.

- **Reinforcement Learning**  
  There is no labeled dataset. An agent learns by interacting with an environment and receiving feedback in the form of rewards, with the objective of maximizing long-term cumulative reward.

This shift from static data to interaction and sequential decision-making is what makes RL feel so different.

## Core Concepts I’ve Been Learning

### Agent and Environment
The **agent** is the learner or decision-maker, while the **environment** is everything the agent interacts with. At each step, the agent takes an action and the environment responds with a new state and a reward.

### Markov Property
A process satisfies the **Markov property** if the future depends only on the present state and action, not the full history:

$$
P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

This assumption forms the basis of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).

### Observation vs State
A **state** contains complete information about the environment, while an **observation** may be partial or noisy. When the agent does not have access to the full state, the problem becomes partially observable.

### Actions
Actions are the choices available to the agent. The action space can be discrete (e.g., move left/right) or continuous (e.g., steering angles or velocities).

### Rewards and Discounting
Instead of maximizing immediate rewards, reinforcement learning focuses on maximizing **discounted cumulative reward**:

$$
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k r_{t+k}
$$

Here, $\gamma \in [0,1]$ is the discount factor that controls how much future rewards matter.

### Exploration vs Exploitation Trade-off
The agent must balance:
- **Exploration**: trying new actions to discover better outcomes
- **Exploitation**: choosing actions that are already known to give good rewards

Finding the right balance is one of the central challenges in RL.

### Policy
A **policy** $\pi(a \mid s)$ defines the agent’s behavior by mapping states to actions or action probabilities.

### Policy-Based vs Value-Based Methods
- **Policy-based methods** directly learn the policy.
- **Value-based methods** learn value functions and derive the policy from them.

### State-Value and Action-Value Functions
The **state-value function** measures how good a state is under a policy:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t = s]
$$

The **action-value function** evaluates how good taking a specific action in a state is:

$$
Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a]
$$

### Epsilon-Greedy Strategy
A simple exploration strategy where:
- With probability $\varepsilon$, the agent chooses a random action
- With probability $1 - \varepsilon$, it chooses the best-known action

### On-Policy vs Off-Policy Algorithms
- **On-policy** methods learn from actions taken by the current policy
- **Off-policy** methods learn from actions generated by a different policy, enabling experience reuse

### Monte Carlo and Temporal Difference Learning
- **Monte Carlo methods** learn from complete episodes
- **Temporal Difference (TD) methods** learn incrementally using bootstrapping:

$$
V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha \big(r_{t+1} + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\big)
$$

### Q-Learning
Q-learning is a popular off-policy TD method that learns the optimal action-value function:

$$
Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \big(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\big)
$$

### Q-Table
In small and discrete environments, $Q(s,a)$ values can be stored explicitly in a table. This approach quickly becomes infeasible as the state space grows.

### Deep Q-Learning (DQN)
Deep Q-learning replaces the Q-table with a neural network that approximates $Q(s,a)$, allowing reinforcement learning to scale to high-dimensional state spaces.

### Experience Replay
Past experiences $(s, a, r, s')$ are stored and replayed during training. This breaks correlation between samples and improves learning stability.

### Double DQN
Double DQN reduces overestimation bias by decoupling action selection and evaluation, leading to more stable and reliable learning.

## Closing Thoughts

Reinforcement learning feels like a shift from *predicting* to *deciding*. Instead of asking “what is the correct output?”, the question becomes “what should I do next?” and that change in framing makes all the difference.

This is just the beginning, but even at this stage, RL already feels like a powerful and conceptually rich framework. I’m excited to explore policy gradients, actor–critic methods, and more advanced ideas next.
